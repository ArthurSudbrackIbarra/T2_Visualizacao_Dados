\section{Analysis of Results}

We used the tool to look at the four questions raised in the first part of the work.
This section reports what the visualization shows for each one, using the public SIH/SUS data through the interface described above.
The examples below use complete years, since the latest year in the extract is still missing part of its final months.

\subsection{Patient export and regional hubs}

Most hospital care is local, but a large share of patients still cross a municipal border.
In our data about one admission in three happens outside the patient's home municipality, close to 34 percent between 2015 and 2019.
The flow map turns this share into arcs and shows where the patients go.

The ranking panel answers which cities sustain the care of a region.
Figure~\ref{fig:ranking} shows Rio Grande do Sul, where Porto Alegre attracts far more patients than any other city, followed by Passo Fundo and Santa Maria.
The other side of the toggle lists Viam\~{a}o, Alvorada, and Gravata\'{\i}, municipalities whose residents depend on care produced elsewhere.
A small set of hubs therefore carries the demand of many surrounding towns.

The map shows the same balance for a single specialty.
Figure~\ref{fig:contrast} draws facial surgery for the two extreme cities of the ranking.
Porto Alegre is covered by incoming blue arcs from across the state and from other states, while Viam\~{a}o shows mostly outgoing orange arcs.
The same picture answers the first question, since the arcs and their width report the export of patients between places for a chosen specialty.

\subsection{Gaps by specialty}

A city can be a hub for one specialty and dependent for another.
Figure~\ref{fig:gap} shows Santa Maria, a hub in the interior of Rio Grande do Sul, under two specialties in 2023.
For cardiology the city received 3{,}391 admissions against 2{,}061 from its own residents, a positive balance of 1{,}330 and an export rate of 1 percent.
For gastroenterology the same city received 281 against 405 residents, a negative balance of 124 and an export rate of 45 percent.

Changing only the specialty filter flips Santa Maria from a blue hub reached by incoming arcs to a red municipality whose residents leave for other cities.
This is the kind of care gap that the second question asks about, and the tool locates it for any specialty with enough records.
The specialty field favors bed-based specialties, so a few areas such as oncology are under-reported and harder to read this way.

\subsection{Seasonality}

The monthly balance of resident and incoming patients is specific to each city.
Figure~\ref{fig:seasonality} shows six municipalities across the twelve months of 2021.
Vit\'{o}ria da Conquista is the most common case, where residents and incoming patients rise and fall together.
Cear\'{a}-Mirim shows both groups climbing sharply from March, a clear seasonal peak that a hospital could prepare for.

The other four cities show patterns that do not repeat elsewhere.
In Muri\'{a}e the incoming patients peak several times while residents stay flat.
In Gurupi the resident and incoming peaks alternate through the year.
In Luzi\^{a}nia and Teut\^{o}nia a rise in residents lines up with a drop in incoming patients, which suggests that residents are served first when local demand grows; Teut\^{o}nia also peaks in winter.

Because the shape changes so much between cities, a single national rule does not hold.
In most cities residents and incoming patients move together, so seasonal peaks are usually not caused by a sudden rise in outside patients.
The value of the monthly view is local: a manager can read the curve for one city and plan for its own peak, which is the practical answer to the fourth question.
