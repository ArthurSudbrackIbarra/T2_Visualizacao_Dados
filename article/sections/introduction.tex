Brazil's Unified Health System (SUS) guarantees every resident the right to publicly funded hospital care.
In practice, hospitals and medical specialties are distributed unevenly across the territory, so a large number of patients are admitted far from where they live.
Knowing where these patients go, and for which specialties, is valuable for planning hospital capacity and for identifying regions that depend on care produced elsewhere.

The Hospital Information System of SUS (SIH/SUS), published by DATASUS, records every publicly funded admission.
It is a general-purpose dataset, since each record carries more than one hundred fields, among them diagnoses, procedures, costs, length of stay, intensive care use, and patient demographics.
The same record also stores the patient's municipality of residence and the municipality of the hospital, so a single admission already describes a movement between two places.
This work sets the clinical and financial fields aside and concentrates on that movement, because the origin and destination together expose problems of access that the other fields do not show directly.
We chose this angle because the flow of patients can reveal planning problems, bottlenecks, gaps in care, and regional overload.

Our extract holds more than 180 million admissions from 2008 to 2023.
The official route to this public data is the DATASUS tabulation portal, which returns the records as configurable tables.
At this scale a table is a weak instrument for the flow of patients: reading movement among thousands of municipalities from rows and columns is impractical, and aggregate counts can conceal where a region depends on outside care.
This is the gap that our work addresses.

We present an interactive flow map of these hospitalization flows.
Each arc links a patient's residence to the hospital municipality, and its width grows with the number of admissions.
The view operates at three levels, from the whole country down to a state and then a single municipality, and it can be filtered by specialty, year, and month.
We do not claim to identify every gap or bottleneck in the system.
Our contribution is the tool itself, a visualization that makes these patterns visible where tables struggle, together with examples that show it in use.

The design supports three themes of analysis that motivated the project:
(a) gaps in care, where the residents of a region must travel for a given specialty, which can show a government where attention is needed;
(b) hub cities that concentrate the care of a whole region, where the concentration of demand may signal overload or a bottleneck for planning;
(c) the effect of seasonality, that is, whether peaks fall on residents or on incoming patients, how different cities absorb them, and whether a peak month raises the share of incoming patients or the system serves residents first.
The remaining sections describe the data preparation and the design of the visualization, and then present examples for each theme.
