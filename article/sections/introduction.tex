Brazil's Unified Health System (SUS) guarantees every resident the right to publicly funded hospital care.
In practice, hospitals and medical specialties are distributed unevenly across the territory, so a large number of patients are admitted far from where they live.
Knowing where these patients go, and for which specialties, is valuable for planning hospital capacity and for identifying regions that depend on care produced elsewhere.

The Hospital Information System of SUS (SIH/SUS), published by DATASUS, records every publicly funded admission.
It is a general-purpose dataset, since each record carries more than one hundred fields, among them diagnoses, procedures, costs, length of stay, intensive care use, and patient demographics.
The same record also stores the patient's municipality of residence and the municipality of the hospital, so a single admission already describes a movement between two places.

This work sets the clinical and financial fields aside and concentrates on that movement.
Origin and destination together expose problems of access that the other fields do not show directly.
The flow of patients can point to gaps in care, bottlenecks at regional hubs, and local overload.

Our extract holds 183 million admissions from 2008 to 2023.
The official route to this public data is the DATASUS tabulation portal, which returns the records as configurable tables.
A table is a weak instrument for this question.
Reading movement among thousands of municipalities from rows and columns is impractical, and the aggregate counts a table reports can hide where a region depends on care produced elsewhere.

We present an interactive flow map of these hospitalization flows.
Each arc links a patient's residence to the hospital municipality, and its width grows with the number of admissions.
The view operates at three levels, from the whole country down to a state and then a single municipality, and it can be filtered by specialty, year, and month.

We do not claim to identify every gap or bottleneck in the system, nor do we evaluate the tool with users.
This paper is a design and system description: we explain how the visualization is built to make these patterns easier to see than a table allows, and we present case studies that illustrate its use.
A study with health managers is left for future work.

The design supports three themes of analysis that motivated the project.
The first is gaps in care, where the residents of a region must travel for a given specialty, which can show a government where attention is needed.
The second is hub cities that concentrate the care of a whole region, where the demand they absorb may signal overload or a bottleneck for planning.
The third is seasonality: whether a monthly peak falls on residents or on incoming patients, and whether a city serves its own residents first or absorbs an influx from elsewhere.
The remaining sections describe the data preparation and the design of the visualization, and then present a case study for each theme.
