\section{Proposed Visualization}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Data preparation}

The raw SIH/SUS extract is too large to query while the user waits.
It holds the admissions in a 26\,GB table, and a single aggregation over one year already takes tens of seconds.
To keep interaction fast, we move all of the heavy work to an offline step that runs once.

This step makes one pass over the fact table.
It joins each admission to the municipality where the patient lives and to the municipality of the hospital, then groups the rows by origin, destination, specialty, year, and month.
Municipality codes follow the six-digit IBGE scheme and resolve against a municipality table that carries latitude and longitude.
The window from 2008 to 2023 covers 183{,}274{,}245 admissions.
No record is dropped at this stage, since every admission carries a valid municipality code; only 19 of the 5{,}589 municipalities (0.3 percent) lack coordinates, so the few flows that touch them are not drawn on the map.
The specialty used throughout is the bed-specialty field of SIH/SUS (\texttt{ESPEC}), whose coverage is uneven, a limitation we return to in the Conclusion.

The pass produces three aggregate tables, summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}.
The flow table, with 13.5 million rows, keeps one row per origin, destination, specialty, year, and month, and feeds the arcs.
Two per-municipality tables, one by year and one by year and month, store the admissions each place receives and generates; they feed the ranking, while the monthly resident and incoming series are read back from the flow table.

\subsection{The flow map}

We draw each hospitalization flow as a curved arc from the patient's residence to the hospital municipality.
The width of the arc grows with the number of admissions, so the thickest arcs mark the busiest routes.
Color marks the direction relative to the place in focus, with arcs that leave a region drawn in orange and arcs that arrive drawn in blue.

A choropleth sits behind the arcs and fills each state according to a metric chosen by the user.
The tool offers five metrics, shown for the whole country in Figure~\ref{fig:metrics}.
The default is the \emph{attraction balance}: the admissions a place receives minus the admissions its own residents generate, on a diverging blue-to-red scale, where blue marks a net attractor and red a place that sends away more patients than it receives.
The \emph{export rate} is the share of a place's resident admissions that are treated in another municipality, on a yellow-to-red scale.
Two metrics report absolute volume, the \emph{admissions received} on a blue scale and the \emph{admissions exported} on an orange scale.
The fifth, a \emph{per-capita rate}, divides the admissions received by the local population and multiplies by one thousand, on a purple scale; it draws on IBGE population figures and is available for 2008 to 2021, the years those figures cover.
City nodes appear as circles sized by the number of admissions that pass through them, over a dark basemap that keeps the arcs and nodes distinct.

The arc colors and the choropleth scales are independent encodings, and the legend states which is active: orange and blue mark arc direction, while the choropleth uses its own per-metric scale and is darkened so the brighter arcs stay distinct.

\subsection{Three levels of detail}

The map follows the principle of overview first, then zoom and filter, then details on demand~\cite{Shneiderman1996EyesHaveIt}.
It opens at the national level, where the 27 federative units (26 states and the Federal District) are the nodes and the arcs show flows between them.
Clicking a state opens the state level, where its municipalities become the nodes and the arcs show flows inside the state and toward other states.
Clicking a city opens the city level, which shows every flow that touches that city across the country.
Figure~\ref{fig:drilldown} shows the three levels for Rio Grande do Sul.
A breadcrumb at the top records the path and lets the user step back, and the camera flies to each new selection.

\subsection{Filters and animation}

Filters narrow the data without leaving the map.
The user can pick a medical specialty, a range of years, and a single month, and can change the metric used for the choropleth.
A direction control limits the arcs to those leaving or arriving at the focus, which removes clutter when a city has many connections.
A further option plays the twelve months in sequence, so seasonal change appears as motion on the map.

\subsection{Analytical panels}

Two side panels sit next to the map, both visible in Figure~\ref{fig:drilldown}.
The first ranks municipalities by the same attraction balance used for the choropleth.
A toggle reverses the order between the places that attract the most patients and the places that most depend on others.

The second panel plots a monthly time series that separates resident admissions from incoming ones.
This shows whether a seasonal peak comes from local patients or from people arriving from elsewhere.
The interface is in Portuguese, where this panel labels the two series \emph{Residentes} and \emph{Migrantes}; we call them resident and incoming patients in this paper.

Selecting a row in either panel moves the map to that municipality, so a reading in a panel and the matching shape on the map stay linked.
